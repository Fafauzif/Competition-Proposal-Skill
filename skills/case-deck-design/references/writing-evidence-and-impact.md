# Writing, evidence and impact

## Preserve the user's argument

Use the current MD pack as the starting point for claims, strategy, solution mechanisms and model assumptions. Improve expression and sequence without inventing a new case. Distinguish three interventions:

- **Presentation edit:** shorten a sentence, improve its logic, choose a more explanatory chart, or move details to the right place. Do this within scope.
- **Evidence correction:** find an incorrect denominator, unsupported causal wording or inconsistent calculation. Verify the source, explain the discrepancy and correct only authorized content. Flag locked-slide discrepancies separately.
- **Strategic change:** add a major feature, change the target segment, replace a recommendation or introduce a new economic driver. Propose this explicitly for the user's decision; do not quietly embed it in a polished slide.

External research can verify a source or supply a requested benchmark. Use the environment's browsing rules and prefer primary sources. Do not expand a design task into a broad research project or imply that external material is the user's thesis. Ask for raw files when a missing input materially blocks a claim. Nonblocking missing inputs can remain clearly labelled.

## Write so the story is readable

Use plain, natural professional English. Someone reading only the main headlines and numbered subheadlines should understand the argument. Give each one a real point. Analysis titles normally state a supported insight; solution titles can describe what the solution allows people to do.

| Weak copy | Better approach |
| --- | --- |
| "Survey findings" | State what the survey establishes about the decision |
| "48% success rate" | Explain what people understood or failed to understand, with scope |
| "Solution 2: Mechanism" | Name the system and the practical action it enables |
| "Drive loyalty through an ecosystem" | Explain which benefit and future intention lead to another decision |
| "Improve transparency" | Explain what cost, condition or next step becomes visible |

Introduce the practical problem before research mechanics. For example: "Extra charges can change which offer is affordable. We asked respondents to calculate the round-trip total, including baggage on both legs." This is a style example, not evidence for a future case. Use the actual current-case sample and finding.

Use short connected sentences, concrete actors/actions, selective bolding and brief implications. Prefer "what's included" to "inclusions" and plain language about finding or choosing a product to vague funnel jargon. Avoid generic consulting filler, unexplained abbreviations, inflated claims and repeating chart values as the supposed insight. Do not standardize every paragraph into the same three-part rhythm.

Prefer one-line subheadlines when possible; two lines can be appropriate. Do not compress text to obtain a one-line heading. Rephrase or adjust layout before changing typography. Keep enough explanation to establish what the feature is, why it addresses the barrier and how it works. Adding explanation means replacing ambiguous labels with useful sentences, not filling all white space.

## Distinguish evidence types

Keep a claim ledger with source locator, exact count, denominator, unit, timeframe, selection/sample definition and caveat. Classify each claim:

| Type | Appropriate wording |
| --- | --- |
| Observed dataset result | "In the sampled results…" |
| Respondent report/intention | "Respondents said…" or "would…" |
| Analyst-defined comparison | "Using the selected benchmark…" |
| Inferred cause | "This suggests…" with its evidence and uncertainty |
| Proposed mechanism | "The proposed flow would…" |
| Modelled outcome | "Under the stated assumptions…" |
| External benchmark | "The provider reports…" with relevance and limitations |

Do not merge sample sizes across datasets. Preserve distinctions between selected reviews and failure rates, hypothetical intentions and behavior, search estimates and actual purchases, and a selected schedule comparison and typical trip needs. Do not turn before/after timing availability into proven demand or revenue.

## Issue trees that communicate diagnosis

Use a problem → subproblem → supported cause structure when the case calls for an issue tree. Keep the central challenge specific to the user's case. Reuse a suitable donor's native branches and evidence treatment, then adjust branch count to the analysis.

Attach short quantitative evidence to the relevant leaf, preserving its denominator and scope. A data point can prove an observed barrier while only supporting an inferred explanation. Label inferred causes instead of declaring every symptom a root cause. Do not fabricate causal certainty to satisfy a visual hierarchy.

Make the diagnosis the main visual; strategy contribution can sit in a compact strip or adjacent region. Do not force one-to-one arrows between diagnostic lenses and solutions when a solution addresses several barriers or when repeat-use mechanisms are less proven. Use evidence already shown when synthesizing, rather than adding an unplanned dataset to fill a branch.

## Explain solutions without a fixed template

Across each solution, answer:

1. What is the proposed service/product/process, and who uses it?
2. Which observed problem or inferred cause does it address?
3. What happens, in which order, and who is responsible for each action?
4. What should improve, why, and how will it be measured?
5. Where available, what external example supports the mechanism and what does not transfer?

Let these answers occupy a composition suited to the content: a prominent proposal screen with explanation, a split traveler/agent workspace, a handoff diagram with an impact calculation, or a benefit-to-next-decision journey. Keep the coverage consistent while varying the arrangement. Do not impose a solution-pair count unless requested.

Every feature should connect to a practical barrier. A screenshot alone does not explain that connection. Add short callouts to the exact visible feature, plus enough explanation for a reader unfamiliar with the product. Distinguish closely related actions: approval versus payment, support versus funding, a case update versus resolution, or engagement versus incremental repeat use.

## Quantify expected impact honestly

Prefer a transparent causal calculation to an unexplained percentage. For each modelled benefit, identify:

- Eligible audience or transactions in scope.
- Current baseline and its source; mark unknowns.
- Mechanism changing a specific behavior or operating metric.
- Explicit scenario/target values and time horizon.
- Resulting count, revenue, contribution or cash, with units.
- Pilot measure that tests whether the mechanism caused the change.

Illustrative algebra (not default assumptions):

`Incremental bookings = eligible offers × (test conversion − counterfactual conversion)`

`Incremental contribution = incremental bookings × contribution per booking − incremental operating/benefit costs`

`Incremental repeat bookings = eligible completed trips × (repeat rate in test − comparable control repeat rate)`

Do not substitute all future repeat bookings for incremental repeat bookings. Do not count the same booking as an Access benefit and a Return benefit. Do not multiply a self-reported intention by the full addressable market and present the result as a forecast. Where inputs are absent, show the model structure and specific missing input rather than inventing precise improvement percentages.

Benchmarks support plausibility, not transfer of numerical impact. Report provider case studies as provider-reported. Identify which mechanism is analogous, such as approval routing or accountable case management, even when the industry differs. Keep sample, implementation context and attribution limits. Do not claim that similar software proves production integration or adoption.

## Finance, implementation and risk

Use supplied financial work. Reconcile executive-summary figures with financial slides. Keep revenue, gross margin/contribution, profit and cash distinct. Include build costs, recurring costs, relevant drivers, scenarios and break-even/payback only where inputs support them. Specify units and time basis. Reuse a donor's chart arrangement while building native charts from the current model; preserve workbook formulas when provided.

Use an editable Gantt or other suitable rollout visual to show owners, dependencies, milestones and expansion gates. Separate what a frontend demo shows from operational requirements such as payment authority, integrations, staffing and data controls. Avoid invented precise targets; label proposals.

Select a few material risks arising from the actual solution. Link each to a concrete mitigation and owner when known or proposed. A donor risk matrix is a visual structure, not evidence that all its risks matter. End with a specific recommendation and decision requested, consistent with the user's case.
