# Research, Evidence, and Citations

Start with the problem, decision, and claim at issue. Read the current brief and the relevant evidence the team has already gathered. An interview, market dataset, site observation, and review corpus can answer different questions. Use the full relevant evidence base. A new search should close a material gap or verify a claim.

## Build and check the evidence

1. Classify each decisive claim as external fact, documented observation, reasoned interpretation, proposed design, calculation under assumptions, target, or achieved result.
2. Match the evidence to the claim's meaning, date, population, location, unit, and denominator. Read the relevant section, table, or figure in context. Verify the exact bibliographic record and the source's support independently of the narrative around it.
3. Prefer original studies, peer-reviewed syntheses, official statistics and rules, and primary documentation suited to the question. Prefer recent relevant literature for theory and current evidence. Keep a foundational source where its contribution warrants it. Browse to verify current, specialized, material, or high-stakes facts. A search snippet establishes little about a study's methods or the size of an effect.
4. Use the studies to develop the argument. Explain what they jointly establish, where they differ, and which unresolved question matters to this proposal. Preserve the distinction between correlation, causal evidence, a mechanistic inference, and interpretation.
5. Keep a private record linking each consequential claim to a source locator, calculation, or named assumption and the intended location in the submission. Ask the user before adopting a material assumption about scope, interpretation, money, or conclusions.

Read [claim and source checks](references/claim-and-source-checks.md) for special cases, quantitative verification, and sample register fields.

## Cite what readers can trace

Use APA 7 where applicable, subject to the competition's citation style. Cite the authoritative authors or institution, year, and meaningful document identity. Include stable DOI or official links in references where available. Provide the necessary reader-visible scope next to the claim or exhibit. Link a sentence to the specific appendix exhibit when it has material supporting detail there.

Keep internal sourcing out of the audience artifact. Upload filenames, scratch documents, tool IDs, and worksheet cell coordinates are working provenance. Supplied public or audience-authorized materials can appear under their published title or accurate description when independently traceable or intended for the reader. An internal working note, including a draft concept brief, is never promoted to published evidence or made into a pseudo-reference. If a quantitative claim relies solely on internal material, either use a verified public basis, present transparent author-approved primary analysis using an appropriate audience-facing method and scope, make it an explicitly stated planning assumption where warranted, or leave out the unsupported claim and disclose the consequential gap outside the submission. Preserve useful reasoning through a qualitative or visibly schematic exhibit when that is enough.

Describe a proposed mechanism directly. Label model inputs and outputs as planning or team assumptions, projections, or scenarios where readers need the distinction. Present measured outcomes only when actually documented. Keep the difference between a proxy and the eventual outcome explicit. Show real approvals, participant data, contracts, live integrations, or deployed status only with evidence and permission to include them.

## Close the research pass

Check source identity and scope, arithmetic and formulas, citation-to-reference consistency, stable links, and the match between prose, figures, and supporting exhibits. Retain private verification notes. If access or verification is incomplete, state the affected claim and limit in the handoff. Claim only checks that were actually run.
