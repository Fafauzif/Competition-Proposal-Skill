# Proposal Strategy and Argument

Make the case around the decision the reader has to make. Build the full logic before deciding what can move to supporting exhibits.

## Establish the argument

1. Frame the problem in the audience's unit of decision. Identify who experiences the problem, what they do today, which constraint changes their result, and why it matters. A useful finding changes the design or the decision. Avoid reading a correlation, a complaint sample or a scenario as a measured causal effect.
2. Tie each meaningful part of the proposal to a supported bottleneck, benefit or obligation. Keep rival explanations visible when they could change what to recommend. Compare real alternatives with a consistent scope and account for where the existing approach works.
3. State the proposal in plain language. Identify what it is, who uses it, where and when it applies, why they need it, and how the main parts work together. Write one coherent paragraph or a short thesis as the format calls for. Introduce function before acronyms and feature names.
4. Explain how it works by following one representative use. Show the action, reason, ownership, handoff, exception when material, and completion. Then develop each component in the sequence it affects. A component deserves depth if the reader needs it to understand the solution or assess a constraint.
5. State the outcome and the mechanism connecting it to the proposal. Define what counts. A request can start a process, a fulfilled job can generate contribution, and a completed study can support a decision. Link substantive claims to evidence or explicit assumptions and show how the baseline affects impact.
6. Backcast from the complete operation. Identify the resources, commitments, economics and implementation needed to deliver it, with milestones where useful. Include testing when the brief requires it or when the result would change a consequential decision.

## Sell the finished idea

Write the experience in the present tense as part of the proposed design. Keep deployments, integrations, partners, approvals and achieved results factual. State thresholds and projections as such at the point of interpretation. A demonstration illustrates or demonstrates only the behavior actually visible. Avoid a recurring disclaimer around every feature. Let the proposal read as a recommendation with an operating model and a route to delivery.

Use the structure of the current format. A BCC recommendation can lead with the decision, supported insights or named pillars. An innovation essay can establish urgency and evidence in the introduction, then present the complete idea in Gagasan. A BPC submission can use a conventional offer, market, operations and finance structure. Preserve the controlling brief and the user's chosen scope.

Read [operating examples](references/operating-examples.md) to see how to move from features to an argument. Open Writing and Editing for voice and heading choices. Open Implementation, Impact, and Feasibility for backcasting and the financial model.

Before delivery, read the main submission without opening the appendix. A judge should find the proposal, understand what changes in practice, assess why it is worth doing, and see what the proposal expects to achieve. Use exhibits for close inspection and supporting depth.
